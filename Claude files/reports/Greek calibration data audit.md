# Greek data expose the bank block's mismatched definitions

The global-projection calibration on branch `calibration` is only partly disciplined by Greek and German data. Five numbers come from Greek or German sources: the 14.4% / 6.5% own-sovereign exposures (EBA, Dec-2010), leverage 8, the 0.1%-per-quarter priced default probability at rest, and the 45% recovery. Leverage 8 traces only to a hard-coded table string. The recovery matches the face-value PSI haircut but not the present-value one. Bocola's Italian estimates supply most of the rest: rho_s, sigma_s, the bond decay delta_b, the Frisch elasticity, alpha, ksi and the deposit rate. Another group has no source at all: country size 8, home bias 0.85, the trade elasticity 0.5, the Bohn root 0.93, G = 0, the household income process, the banker exit rate f = 0.08 and the TPI key 0.071. Where data exist, the largest disagreements are structural, not cosmetic. Leverage and exposure are measured on different denominators, so the model's sovereign book is **1.15x bank net worth against 2.39x in the EBA data**. The model's banks finance the whole capital stock, which makes bank assets about 2.6x annual GDP, household deposits about 2.2x GDP (data: 0.85–0.93x) and total D debt about 51% of GDP (data: 148%). The implied Bohn coefficient is about 0.27 (euro-area panels: 0.03–0.05). The TPI key does not reproduce its own stated source. None of these numbers is final, however, because the working-tree calibration does not yet hit its own six rest-point targets: the **D wedge sits at ~2 bp against a 100 bp target and D leverage at 7.62**. Every choice the data leave open is set out below as a decision for you, with numbers. This report covers the global model only. The SSJ calibration differs (for example recovery 0.30 at `calibration/ssj.py:390`), and its numbers are not comparable.

## Three balance-sheet mismatches drive most of the disagreement

The first mismatch is the denominator. The EBA exposure target is **face value over total assets**. Leverage 8, however, sits near the EBA *total-book* measure (7.21) or the unsourced 7.98, and well below **TA/CT1 = 16.56**. The data's 14.4% therefore implies 14.4% × 16.56 = **2.39x CT1** in sovereign bonds, while the model's identical 14.4% applied to leverage 8 delivers only **1.15x net worth**. On the bond-loss channel that Bocola's mechanism runs through, the model's D bank is half as exposed as Greek banks were. The IMF's independent figure (45 bn GGBs against 22 bn core capital, about 2.0x) confirms the data side ([IMF CR 11/351](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/scr/2011/cr11351.pdf)). The validation numbers line up with this. The model's repricing at the 9.1% yield costs roughly 25% of net worth (computed at the SS price), against the EBA ladder's pure-repricing 2011 loss of 39.7% of CT1. The 55% haircut costs the "63% direct bond loss" in the memory file, against a realised PSI loss of 1.71x CT1 gross and 1.44x net ([Hardouvelis & Vayanos 2023](https://www.lse.ac.uk/Hellenic-Observatory/Assets/Documents/Publications/GreeSE-Papers/GreeSE-No180.pdf)). D-bank insolvency in default, which the 15% floor now absorbs, is therefore what the data show, not a solver pathology. The numerator mismatch is smaller but real: the model measures Q_bD·b_DD at market value, and the Greek book traded at **77% of face** at end-2010, so the market-value analogue of 14.4% is **11.1%**.

The second mismatch is scale. Every unit of capital is bank-financed, so with r_k + δ = 3.05% per quarter the SS capital stock is 8.2 quarterly GDP, and bank assets are about **10.3 quarterly GDP (2.6x annual GDP)**. EBA Greek-bank total assets were **1.69x GDP** and all Greek MFIs **2.9x** ([IMF CR 11/351](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/scr/2011/cr11351.pdf)). Bank net worth is about 32% of annual GDP, against CT1 of 10.2% (EBA) or capital and reserves of 20.9% (MFIs). Deposits, which the household block must hold because β clears the deposit market, come to about **224% of GDP against 85–93%** in Eurostat ([Eurostat nasa_10_f_bs](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/databrowser/view/nasa_10_f_bs/default/table)). The table's 84% household validation will miss by a factor of about 2.7. In Greece, the missing funding came from interbank markets and Eurosystem credit (97.8 bn at Dec-2010), not deposits. The same scale makes the bank-held GGB book about **40% of GDP against 24%**. The table's own "40% bank-held debt" row, labelled "Not matched", happens to coincide with the model figure, and its source is unknown.

The third mismatch is the debt stock and its holders. Only banks hold government bonds in the model, so total D debt is bank-held debt divided by 0.8, about **51% of GDP**. Greece had 148% (2010), 117% of it in securities, and the pre-PSI holder base was far from bank-only: the Eurosystem held 56.7 bn, social-security funds 23.7 bn, Greek banks about 45 bn, and the remainder of the 205.6 bn eligible stock was mostly foreign private holders ([ZTG 2013](https://www.ifo.de/DocDL/cesifo1_wp4333.pdf)). The model cannot match both total debt and bank exposure with its existing holder set. The defensible target is the bank-held stock (GR + DE EBA banks: 27.9% of GDP, 12.7% of it German). That choice also reprices the Bohn rule and the TPI P&L, which both scale with B_gov.

## Conclusion

The audit separates the calibration's problems into two layers. On the surface, individual values are borrowed or unsourced, and most of them can be swapped for a Greek number, as the tables show. Underneath, the bank block's systemic quantities, meaning leverage, exposure, balance-sheet scale and the debt stock, are each matched to a different definition. The resulting model bank holds half the Greek sovereign-to-capital ratio while carrying more than twice the Greek deposit base. Fixing D1 and D2 together, on one denominator, matters more for the pass-through mechanism than any single borrowed parameter: it decides how hard a priced default shock hits net worth, and the existing EBA file already contains every number needed to do it consistently.

Two findings also bear on the open rest-point problem. First, the Greek default process is less persistent and less volatile than Bocola's Italian one, and the log-normal mean of pd under the current rho_s and sigma_s is nearly three times its median. That points to the s-process as a likely contributor to the 181 bp rest-point spread and to the D bank earning a risk premium at rest. Second, the data place the Greek–German credit-cost gap in funding costs rather than intermediation margins. If you choose to follow that, the 100 bp wedge would become a common margin of about 2 pp, and the cross-country gap would move to the deposit-market premium. Any such change should be made only after the six-target search converges at whatever definitions you choose, since the walk shows the D wedge responds non-monotonically to the instrument.
